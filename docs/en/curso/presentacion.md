# Presentation

## Goals

Physics is an empirical discipline: its theories are born from experiments and checked against them. In systems made of an enormous number of particles (of the order of Avogadro's number) real experiments are expensive or impossible to bring into the classroom, and the student loses the empirical view of the subject. **Computational experiments** recover it at low cost and with great conceptual clarity: a model with simple rules, parameters you can touch, and results compared with theory.

This course gathers interactive experiments from four subjects: Statistical Physics, Non-equilibrium Systems, Solid State Physics and Quantum Devices. It is the current version of the course *Interactive Experiments in Complex Physical Systems* created at Universidad Complutense de Madrid in 2002 with two Educational Innovation Projects, whose Java applets stopped working when browsers dropped Java.

## What is new in this version

- **Equation and simulation on the same screen.** Next to every simulation the governing equation is shown with its symbols replaced by the measured values in real time, and the theoretical prediction is drawn over the data. A green or amber indicator tells whether simulation and theory agree.
- **Revised physics.** Every experiment has been audited: errors of the original applets were fixed (from a collision counter divided by four to an activation rate overestimated twenty-five-fold) and every sheet documents the changes.
- **Automatic tests.** Every model has numerical tests checking conservation laws, stationary distributions and known results; every page is tested in a real browser before publication.
- **Bilingual, no installation.** Spanish and English, runs in any modern browser, also on phones, and every experiment can be embedded separately.

## How to follow the course

Each experiment has a simulation with controls, the live equation, and below it the theory in collapsible sections: *what you see*, *physical model*, *algorithm*, *changes with respect to the original applet*, *tests* and *didactic assessment*. Start with the hint under each simulation, change one parameter at a time and watch how the equation and the indicator respond. The recommended bibliography lets you go deeper.

## Who it is for

Students of Physics and Electronic Engineering degrees and of Physics master's programmes taking Statistical Physics, Non-equilibrium or Complex Systems, Solid State Physics and Quantum Devices, and anyone with a second-year physics background who wants to see how macroscopic laws emerge from simple microscopic rules.
